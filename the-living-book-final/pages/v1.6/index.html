The Evolution of Intelligence

From the organisms that survived Earth's harshest environments, to dinosaurs that never became technological, to the possibility of evolving intelligence inside artificial worlds.

The Strongest Life

If by “strongest life ever formed on Earth” you mean organisms with the most extreme physical/biological abilities—surviving heat, radiation, pressure, dehydration, toxins, starvation, etc.—there isn't one universal strongest. Different organisms dominate different environments.

Here’s a good “Earth's biological champions” list:

And there's an interesting deeper answer

If you mean “the strongest form of life that has ever existed”, rather than merely the most resistant organism, the answer becomes much more interesting.

You could construct a hierarchy like:

Extremophiles → ancient microorganisms → multicellular animals → apex predators → humans → technological civilization

At the biological level, microbes are arguably Earth's ultimate survivors. They have endured billions of years of climate changes, mass extinctions, radiation, oceans freezing, atmospheric transformations and catastrophic geological events.

Humans are almost the opposite strategy:

We aren't exceptionally resistant individually. We became exceptionally powerful by modifying our environment.

That distinction—survival through biological robustness vs survival through intelligence/technology—is one of the most fascinating themes in evolution.

First, the key correction

Modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared roughly 300,000 years ago, but human-like intelligence did not suddenly appear at that point. The evolutionary buildup involved millions of years of hominin evolution.

Dinosaurs, meanwhile, dominated terrestrial ecosystems for roughly 165 million years.

So the interesting question is:

If natural selection produced extreme intelligence in humans relatively quickly, why didn't it produce something comparable in dinosaurs over 165 million years?

There are several possible answers.

The Dinosaur Intelligence Question

1. Dinosaurs actually DID evolve increasing intelligence

This is important.

Some dinosaurs had relatively sophisticated brains compared with earlier dinosaurs.

Theropods—including Tyrannosaurus, dromaeosaurs and troodontids—show evidence of increasingly complex nervous systems.

And birds are actually living dinosaurs.

Modern corvids and parrots demonstrate impressive cognitive abilities: tool use, problem solving, memory, planning, social learning, etc.

So evolution did push dinosaur cognition quite far in some branches.

But it didn't produce humans.

2. There is no fossil evidence for a dinosaur technological civilization

This is probably the strongest argument.

If a dinosaur lineage had developed something approaching human technological intelligence, we'd expect at least some indirect evidence.

For example:

  • sophisticated tools
  • deliberate large-scale structures
  • controlled fire
  • extensive modification of landscapes
  • increasingly complex manufactured objects
  • unusual accumulation of artifacts
  • writing/symbolic markings
  • rapidly increasing brain size
  • evidence of extremely complex social organization

We don't have anything remotely comparable.

And this matters because technology leaves traces.

Even if almost all biological evidence disappears over tens of millions of years, a technological species could leave geological signatures.

3. But absence of evidence isn't proof of impossibility

This is where scientists have to be careful.

Suppose an intelligent dinosaur species existed for only 50,000 years and disappeared.

Its civilization could potentially leave almost no recognizable fossil record.

So:

"We haven't found evidence" ≠ "it never happened."

But the longer and larger the civilization, the harder it becomes to hide.

A civilization comparable to ours would probably leave substantial geological evidence.

4. The really fascinating possibility: dinosaurs may have had the evolutionary ingredients

Some dinosaurs possessed traits that could theoretically support further cognitive evolution:

Large brains

Some theropods had relatively large brains for dinosaurs.

Excellent sensory systems

Predatory theropods evolved sophisticated:

  • vision
  • hearing
  • smell
  • balance
  • coordination

Social behavior

Some dinosaurs show evidence consistent with complex social behavior.

Long lifespans

Some large dinosaurs probably lived for many years, giving individuals opportunities to learn.

Birds

This is perhaps the strongest evidence that dinosaurian nervous systems were capable of remarkable cognition.

5. So why didn't intelligence keep accelerating?

This is where evolution gets really interesting.

Evolution does not have a goal of producing intelligence.

If being smarter doesn't substantially increase reproductive success, natural selection doesn't necessarily push intelligence upward.

Consider a hypothetical dinosaur:

Brain A: enough intelligence to hunt effectively.

Brain B: 30% larger brain, requiring substantially more energy.

If Brain B doesn't produce more surviving offspring, selection has little reason to favor it.

Brains are extremely expensive organs metabolically.

Humans essentially went down a positive-feedback pathway:

Intelligence can create a feedback loop: better thinking enables better tools; better tools improve survival and social learning; those advantages can make further intelligence more valuable.

That feedback loop can potentially accelerate evolution.

6. Humans had an unusual combination of traits

This is arguably more important than simply having a large brain.

Humans had:

The unusual combination
Human intelligence became unusually powerful because several traits reinforced one another.
large brainscomplex social groupslanguagecumulative cultureteachingtoolsbetter survivallearning / problem solving

This produces something unusual:

Cumulative cultural evolution

A human doesn't have to reinvent the wheel.

One generation learns something.

The next generation modifies it.

The next improves it again.

After thousands of generations:

Stone tools, metallurgy, engines, computers and spacecraft show what happens when each generation can inherit and improve the previous generation's ideas.

Biological evolution isn't even necessary for every improvement.

That's an enormous advantage.

7. And here's the crazy part about dinosaurs

They actually survived for much longer than mammals have been around in their current forms.

Dinosaurs appeared approximately 230 million years ago.

Non-avian dinosaurs survived until the asteroid impact about 66 million years ago.

That's around:

165 million years of evolutionary experimentation.

During that time they evolved:

  • gigantic herbivores
  • gigantic predators
  • fast runners
  • armored species
  • flying species
  • aquatic-adapted species
  • pack/social behaviors
  • sophisticated sensory systems
  • increasingly bird-like nervous systems

Yet none developed human-level technological intelligence.

That suggests human-like intelligence isn't an inevitable endpoint of evolution.

8. But there's an even more interesting counterargument

You could ask:

What if the asteroid hadn't hit?

We simply don't know.

That's the scientifically honest answer.

Perhaps after another 50 million years:

A troodontid-like lineage becoming increasingly intelligent, then developing tool use and cumulative culture, is biologically conceivable as a thought experiment—but the fossil record does not show that trajectory actually happened.

It's not forbidden by evolutionary theory.

In fact, paleontologist Dale Russell famously proposed a speculative hypothetical dinosaur called the "dinosauroid", imagining what an intelligent troodontid descendant might look like.

But that's a thought experiment, not evidence.

The most important conclusion

Your observation points toward a major idea in evolutionary biology:

Evolution doesn't necessarily move toward greater intelligence, complexity, or technological ability.

Dinosaurs weren't an evolutionary "failure" because they didn't become humans.

They were enormously successful.

They occupied Earth's terrestrial ecosystems for ~165 million years.

Humans appeared extremely recently.

And yet in roughly 300,000 years, Homo sapiens went from stone tools to nuclear physics, genetic engineering and spacecraft.

That's not because evolution was secretly trying to create humans.

It's because a particular combination of intelligence, social learning, language, hands, bipedalism, long childhood, cooperation and cumulative culture created an extraordinary evolutionary feedback loop.

And there's one implication that's especially fascinating:

If intelligence is not inevitable, then the emergence of technological intelligence on Earth may have been a relatively rare evolutionary event—not something that was destined to happen eventually.

That question connects directly to the Fermi paradox and the Great Filter: if technological intelligence is so advantageous once it appears, why don't we see evidence of many other technological species?

Artificial Evolution

1. The basic idea

Instead of a human directly designing, training and selecting the final neural network, the experiment moves the search process into the evolutionary world itself.

you want:

The experiment
Don't design the intelligence. Design the world that makes useful intelligence worth keeping.
01
OrganismsBegin with variation: bodies, sensors and small neural networks.
02
WorldLet food, danger, terrain and scarcity create real consequences.
03
SelectionSurvival and reproduction decide which designs continue.
04
MutationChange weights, connections and eventually the architecture itself.
05
Generation after generationRepeat until patterns emerge that you did not explicitly program.

This is not ordinary machine learning.

You're making evolution itself the learning algorithm.

2. The technology you should look at

The field you want is called:

Neuroevolution

The neural network itself can evolve.

One of the most famous approaches is:

NEAT — NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies

The interesting part of NEAT is that it doesn't just evolve the weights.

It can evolve the architecture.

For example:

Evolving topology
The network can become more structurally capable instead of merely receiving better numbers.
Generation 1Input → Output
Generation 20Input → Hidden → Output
Generation 100Input → Hidden → Hidden → Output

So the evolutionary process can discover:

  • new neurons
  • new connections
  • connection strengths
  • network topology

That is extremely close to what you're imagining when you say:

"linking or forming the required neural networks."
